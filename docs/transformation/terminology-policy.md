# Terminology policy

> Section 13. English and Spanish must be **equivalent editorial versions**, not
> literal machine translations. Every number below is measured by
> `tools/glossary-scan.cjs` against `src/content/data/*.json` and re-checked on each
> build by `tools/check-glossary.cjs`. Nothing here is hand-counted.

## 1. Source-of-truth language

**English is the master language.** Spanish is a first-class edition, not a fallback.

- A new concept, check, Codex entry or lesson is authored in English first.
- The Spanish version is written as prose, not converted sentence by sentence. The two
  are equivalent in meaning and register; a diff between them is not a defect.
- Where the two disagree on a technical claim, **English is correct by definition** and
  the Spanish is the bug.

This is a policy decision, not an observation about the corpus — both editions are
complete at 20,449 strings each.

## 2. Shared vs localized fields

| Field | Language | Why |
|---|---|---|
| `slug`, `id`, `lessonId`, `concept`, `widgetId` | shared | Identifiers are keys, not prose. Translating one breaks every reference to it. |
| `source` | shared | A citation is a URL or a book title. Translating it makes it uncheckable. |
| `code.snippet`, `code.lang` | shared | Code is code. Only `code.caption` and `code.annotations[].note` are localized. |
| `translate`, `canonical`, `spanish_usage`, `avoid`, `aliases` | shared | A terminology *decision* is one decision. `check-glossary.cjs` fails if these differ between the two glossary files. |
| every `I18nText` — `title`, `why`, `explanation`, `keyPoints`, `prompt`, `explain`, `rationale`, `stem`, `caption` | localized | All learner-facing prose. |
| `first_use_explanation` | localized | The definition a learner reads, so it must read naturally. `check-glossary.cjs` fails if the Spanish one is byte-identical to the English — that is an untranslated field, not a coincidence. |

## 3. Glossary

`content/glossary.en.json` and `content/glossary.es.json` — **128 terms**, generated by
`tools/gen-glossary.cjs`. Each term declares the six fields section 13 requires:

```json
{
  "term": "chunking",
  "canonical": "chunking",
  "spanish_usage": "chunking",
  "translate": false,
  "first_use_explanation": "Dividir documentos fuente en piezas indexables; el tamaño y los límites deciden qué se puede encontrar.",
  "aliases": [],
  "avoid": ["fragmentación de trozos", "troceado", "trozado"],
  "usage": { "en": 36, "esCanonical": 29, "englishFormInSpanish": 29, "ratio": 0.81, "verdict": "kept" }
}
```

`usage` is this repo's addition to the spec's shape, and it is why the glossary can be
trusted: it is measured at build time, so `check-glossary.cjs` fails if anyone
hand-edits a count. A glossary asserting uncheckable facts about the content is a
document; one whose facts are re-measured is a gate.

### How `translate` was decided

Not by taste. Per term, the ratio of *Spanish occurrences of the English word* to
*English occurrences*:

| ratio | reading | decision |
|---|---|---|
| >= 0.5 | the Spanish edition already keeps the English word | `translate: false` |
| <= 0.15 | the Spanish edition has its own settled word | `translate: true` |
| in between | two authors made different calls | read the sentences, then decide |

Result: **82 kept in English, 46 localized.** By measured verdict: 76 clearly kept, 31
clearly localized, 20 inconsistent, 1 too rare to call.

The glossary overrules the raw ratio in **12 cases**, each carrying a written `note`:

- **Kept despite an inconsistent ratio** — `commit`, `guardrail`, `tradeoff`, `lock-in`,
  `trunk-based`, `stakeholder`, `CAP`, `AZ`, `shuffle sharding`. The English form is the
  industry standard and the Spanish rival is idiomatic but not a translation.
- **Localized despite a kept ratio** — `load shedding` (`descarte de carga`, 8 vs 7),
  `cold start` (`arranque en frío`, 10 vs 7), `service mesh` (`malla de servicios`). The
  Spanish form narrowly leads, and the policy is to record what the corpus settled on
  rather than overrule 20,000 strings from taste.

### `avoid` — 80 banned renderings

A rendering in `avoid` **fails the build** if it appears in Spanish prose. That is what
makes the glossary enforceable, and it is why the list was built by reading sentences
rather than by counting.

An entry must pass three tests. The first draft banned 120 renderings from intuition;
the validator found 42 of them shipping, and reading those sentences showed roughly half
the bans were wrong:

1. **The rendering must not have its own meaning.** `rendimiento` was banned as a calque
   of `throughput`; its 137 uses mean *performance* — "más aislamiento compra seguridad
   con rendimiento". Banning it would have failed 137 correct sentences. Unbanned for the
   same reason: `respaldo` (backing, in delegation), `expiración` (TTL expiry),
   `capa de datos` (the data tier), `ficha` (a spec sheet / model card), `retroceso` (as
   in `retroceso exponencial`, backoff), `trozo` (an ordinary piece — "un trozo de
   pegamento"), `exhaustividad` (exhaustiveness), `rescate` (rescuing a mentee, not
   retrieval), `límite` (threshold), `cola` (queue), `bloqueo` (a row lock), `búsqueda`
   (a lookup), `inferencia` (inference).
2. **An acronym expansion is never banned.** "objetivo de nivel de servicio (SLO)" *is*
   the `first_use_explanation` section 13 asks for. Banning the expansions of SLO, SLI,
   SLA, ADR, DLQ, MCP, LLM and RAG would forbid the exact practice the glossary exists to
   encourage.
3. **The corpus must not already prefer it.** Where the Spanish form leads, the decision
   flips instead — the three cases above.

`principal` was excluded from the glossary entirely. It measures ratio 2.09 — on paper
the most strongly "kept" term in the corpus — and is in fact the ordinary Spanish
adjective for *main*. A false friend is not a glossary entry.

### Known debt

`tools/glossary-baseline.txt` lists **7** banned renderings that already ship, as
`term|rendering`. A listed pair warns; an unlisted pair fails. **The list may only
shrink** — adding a line to make a build pass is forbidden, because that converts a
defect into a permission.

The current 7 are genuine terminology debt, each verified by reading its sentences:
`troceado` (a calque of chunking), `primeros k`, `presupuesto de errores` (plural
drift), `sin culpables`, `privilegio mínimo` and `menor privilegio` (word-order drift on
one phrase), `llamado de herramientas`.

## 4. Fallback

There is none, by design.

- Both editions are complete: every `I18nText` has a non-empty `en` and `es`.
  `tools/check-prose.cjs` fails on an empty side, so a missing translation cannot ship
  and be quietly papered over with English.
- `src/i18n/config.ts` has no runtime fallback chain. A missing string would render
  empty rather than switch language mid-paragraph — the louder and therefore better
  failure.
- The one deliberate exception is code: `code.snippet` is shared, so a Spanish reader
  sees English identifiers. That is correct — the code is the artifact under discussion.

## 5. Review state

`reviewState: "reviewed"` in both glossary files. What that claims, precisely:

- Every term's `translate` decision was measured against the corpus, and every override
  of the measurement carries a written `note`. **50 of 128** terms have one.
- The scanner reads **all 8** content data files. It read 5 until `tests/glossary.test.ts`
  compared its list against the directory: `builds.json` and the two assessment files
  (`ai-l5.json`, `general-l5.json`) were exempt, hiding 781 learner-facing Spanish strings
  and one real calque (`pipeline de trozeo`, since fixed). A validator's file list is part
  of its claim, so it is now asserted rather than trusted.
- Every `avoid` entry was checked against shipped Spanish prose. 45 conflicts were found
  and resolved — by unbanning where the rendering was a real word, and by baselining the
  7 that are real debt.
- `tools/selftest-glossary.cjs` (**24 checks**) verifies the validator itself: 20 defect
  replays — a hand-edited count, a deleted required field, a stale baseline line, the two
  files disagreeing about one decision — plus 4 correct-content fixtures that must be
  accepted.

What it does **not** claim: that a native reviewer has read all 20,449 Spanish strings.
Two Spanish regressions were introduced and caught during this transformation, so the
prose is machine-checked, not human-certified. `check-prose.cjs` and
`check-glossary.cjs` are what stand behind the Spanish edition today.

## 6. Voice

The spec's voice rules, restated as the ones a check can hold:

- professional, direct, clear — not academic, not promotional, not condescending;
- technically precise: `cost` is a bound or a figure, never an adjective;
- controlled sentence length, **one main idea per paragraph** — enforced by
  `tools/check-prose.cjs`;
- a claim carries a source, and a figure carries a unit.

## 7. Running the checks

```bash
node tools/gen-glossary.cjs      # regenerate both files (never hand-edit them)
node tools/check-glossary.cjs    # enforce against shipped content — in `npm run content:check`
node tools/selftest-glossary.cjs # attack the validator — in `npm run gates:selftest`
node tools/glossary-scan.cjs <term>...   # ad-hoc: how does the corpus treat this term?
```
